About

Climate and energy data science, grounded in engineering practice

I'm a graduate researcher in Earth and Environmental Engineering at Columbia University, where I build data-driven tools at the intersection of climate and energy: forecasting wind along transmission corridors to support dynamic line rating, and using machine learning to overcome sparse ocean observations in climate models.

Before and alongside graduate school, I spent more than two years as an engineer at Burns & McDonnell, permitting solar, battery storage, and transmission projects and analyzing environmental data for remediation and air-emissions work. That experience keeps my analysis connected to how energy infrastructure is planned, permitted, and regulated.

I hold an M.S. in Earth & Environmental Engineering from Columbia, with a concentration in Sustainable Energy & Carbon and additional focuses on data science, water resources, and climate risk, and a B.S. in Chemical Engineering from Washington University in St. Louis.

Current Work

Research at Columbia University

Applied data science for transmission and wind energy, and for representing air-sea exchange in climate models.

Wind Energy & Dynamic Line Rating

The same wind that drives generation also cools transmission lines and raises their dynamic line rating (DLR), so transfer capacity peaks just as wind output does. I apply spatial analysis to develop a risk-mitigation framework for wind energy along transmission corridors: predicting wind speed between sparse measurement points, forecasting with calibrated confidence intervals, and recommending sensor placements to improve safety and reduce curtailment.

Dynamic Line Rating Spatial Statistics Calibrated Uncertainty Sensor Placement Curtailment

Machine Learning for Air-Sea Fluxes

Scarce oceanographic observations limit how well climate models represent air-sea fluxes. I develop a hybrid physics–machine learning workflow using ensembles of neural networks trained on 3 million synthetic samples, then fine-tuned via transfer learning to ~10,000 in-situ observations. Causal discovery (PCMCI+) illuminates directional relationships between meteorological drivers and fluxes, enabling physically grounded interpretation of the predictions.
